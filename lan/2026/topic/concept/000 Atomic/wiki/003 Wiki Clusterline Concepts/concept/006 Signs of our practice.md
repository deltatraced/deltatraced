A sign here is to be taken as the unit of interpretation and redirection. Contrast this with data, which is the unit of observation.

When we engage with a practice, there are many associated data points. I may observe that my boiler boils water in some `X` amount of minutes, at `T` amount of heat, and so on.

But engaging with a practice also emits signs. When I encounter that the rice pot is overflowing, I draw a judgment, which may be instinctual, to interrupt the situation. This encounter also may prompt me to adjust my cooking practice, and water-to-rice ratio used. Through this encounter, we interpret a sign. We engage with signs through interpretation and judgment. Observables such as the appearance of an overflowing rice pot are the presentation of a sign that is then interpreted towards interruption, followed by longer term explanations for why it had occurred.

When we learn a practice, we are continually engaging with its signs. As a programmer, I encounter many similar situations to the rice pot through user feedback, compiler feedback, errors, discrepancies between intention and result, degree of ambiguity of goal, and so on.

Look too close, and you will see data in all of these things. But data should be correctly interpreted and filtered, and this brings us back to signs of our practice which inform our explanatory model of the world.

Data often comes with a direct and objective means of measurement. Signs are phantom and redirecting; they take effort to interpret, and not all attempts to interpret complete, which can lead to confusion or disorientation.

When a detective reconstructs what must have happened from clues on the scene, they are engaging with signs; and the particular clues in that scene are the data that is interpreted must be generated by their explanation. This correspondence of data to explanation is an interpretive effort which can fail: explanations can be wrong, and explanations may initially make sense and cease to make sense in further consideration.

This indirection of explanations to interpret the seen in terms of the unseen is something we are able to do thanks to our engagement with the signs which allows us to reverse engineer massive opaque data into interrelated and relevant information.

Part of building expertise is learning to recognize signs; to be able to diagnose, reconstruct, track, compose how things are; and then re-imagine how they ought to be. It may be difficult to start; we see mass amounts of data which signify very little, but with practice, we learn to be able to pick up on the decisive singular points that shifts our interpretations to align with our goals.
